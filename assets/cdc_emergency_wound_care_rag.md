## 1. Main safety message

Floodwater and standing water can expose people to infectious diseases,
chemical hazards, and injuries. Prompt first aid can help small wounds
heal and reduce the risk of infection.

## 2. Prevent floodwater from contacting wounds

-   Avoid contact with floodwater if you have an open wound.
-   Keep open wounds as clean as possible.
-   Wash wounds thoroughly with soap and clean water.
-   Cover clean, open wounds with a waterproof bandage to reduce the
    chance of infection.

## 3. Basic wound care

1.  Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and clean water, if available.
2.  Avoid touching the wound with your fingers while treating it. Use
    disposable gloves if available.
3.  Remove jewelry or clothing that obstructs access to the injured body
    part.
4.  If the wound is bleeding, apply direct pressure to help control the
    bleeding.
5.  Once bleeding has stopped, clean the wound:
    -   Look for dirt and foreign objects.
    -   Gently rinse the wound with bottled water or clean running
        water. The infographic says saline solution is preferred if
        available.
    -   Gently clean the skin around the wound with soap and clean
        water.
    -   Pat dry and apply an adhesive bandage or a clean, dry cloth.
6.  Keep the wound clean and covered, and check it regularly.

**Important qualification:** This infographic is a basic public fact
sheet, not a complete protocol for severe bleeding or deep wounds. Do
not delay emergency care to clean a serious wound. If bleeding is
severe, spurting, or does not stop with firm, continuous direct
pressure, seek emergency medical help immediately.

## 4. Do not ignore contaminated wounds, bites, or punctures

The infographic warns that wounds that are not cleaned correctly can
trap bacteria and lead to infection. It advises leaving uncleaned
wounds, bites, and puncture wounds open rather than sealing
contamination inside.

-   Do not attempt to remove a deeply embedded object yourself.
-   Seek medical assessment for deep or dirty puncture wounds, animal
    bites, or wounds containing embedded soil, wood, metal, or other
    material.
-   Ask a health professional whether tetanus vaccination or a booster
    is needed, based on the wound and your vaccination history.

## 5. When to seek medical attention

Seek medical attention as soon as possible if:

-   A foreign object, such as soil, wood, metal, or another object, is
    embedded in the wound.
-   The wound resulted from an animal bite.
-   The wound is a puncture caused by a dirty object.
-   The wound develops signs of infection, such as increasing pain or
    soreness, swelling, redness, drainage, or fever.
-   You develop possible signs of a serious systemic infection,
    including confusion or disorientation, shortness of breath, a fast
    heart rate, fever or shivering, extreme pain or discomfort, or
    clammy or sweaty skin.

If serious symptoms occur, treat the situation as urgent and contact
emergency medical services. Do not rely on an AI assistant to determine
whether sepsis is present.

## 6. Tetanus considerations

A health professional should assess whether a tetanus booster or other
preventive care is needed, particularly after a puncture wound or a
wound contaminated with feces, soil, or saliva. The decision depends on
the person's vaccination history and the nature of the wound.

Do not let an AI system decide that a tetanus shot is unnecessary
without reliable clinical guidance and vaccination information.

## 9. Recommended answer policy

-   For severe bleeding or symptoms suggesting a serious infection,
    prioritize emergency medical care.
-   For bleeding wounds, explain direct pressure clearly and do not
    delay escalation for a serious injury.
-   For floodwater exposure, advise avoiding further exposure and
    cleaning the wound with clean water and soap as soon as possible.
-   Recommend clinical assessment for deep, dirty, puncture, bite, or
    embedded-object wounds.
-   Do not diagnose infection or sepsis, prescribe antibiotics, or make
    definitive tetanus-vaccination decisions.
-   Cite the CDC source in answers and pair it with current Philippine
    Department of Health guidance for local deployment.
