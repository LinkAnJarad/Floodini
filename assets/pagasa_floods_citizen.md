# PAGASA Flood Guide for the Public

Source: PAGASA, Learning Tools: Floods (https://www.pagasa.dost.gov.ph/learning-tools/floods)

## What is a flood?

PAGASA defines a flood as an abnormal, progressive rise in the water level of a stream that may overflow its normal banks and inundate areas that are not normally submerged.

Inundation (areas covered by water that are not normally underwater) happens when water overflows from streams and other bodies of water, or when rainwater accumulates because drainage cannot carry it away.

## Types of flooding

Source: PAGASA, Learning Tools: Floods

### Minor flooding

- Floodwater is usually shallow, and there may be no noticeable flow.
- Flooding can happen without a river overflowing. Excess surface runoff collects in low-lying flat areas.
- Water is usually confined to the river's flood plain, low-lying areas, and depressions in the terrain.

### Major flooding

- Caused by overflowing rivers and lakes, serious breaks in dikes, levees, or dams, uncontrolled releases from reservoirs, or accumulated excess runoff.
- Floodwater covers a wide area and spreads quickly to nearby lower-lying areas.
- Flooding is deep in most affected areas.
- There is a noticeable current as the flood spreads.

### Flash floods

- Most floods take 12 to 24 hours or longer to develop after intense rainfall. A flash flood develops in no more than six hours, often much sooner.
- Flash floods happen in hilly and mountainous terrain where river slopes are steep. Rain that falls far from the river reaches the channel quickly, so water levels rise fast.

## Health risks after a flood

Source: PAGASA, Learning Tools: Floods

- Floods commonly cut off food and drinking water supplies.
- Floods disrupt sanitation and almost always contaminate the water supply.
- Outbreaks of gastro-intestinal diseases frequently follow floods in affected areas.
- Respiratory ailments from exposure are also common.

## Understanding PAGASA flood bulletins

Source: PAGASA, Learning Tools: Floods

PAGASA issues "Flood Bulletins" to the public for river basins where a flood is possible. Bulletins are issued only when there is a potential flooding situation, and are prepared twice daily (for release at 5 a.m. and 5 p.m.) so people can act before leaving for work in the morning or before going to sleep at night. They are passed on through agencies such as OCD and DSWD, and through radio and television.

There are three categories, from lowest to highest:

1. **Flood Outlook.** States the present water situation and alerts people in the river basin that conditions may worsen, for example a gradual and continuous rise in water level. It signals a possibility of flooding, not a certainty.
2. **Flood Advisory.** States that a flood situation is imminent. It includes suggested actions for residents and the community. It is issued when the situation worsens further, and also when conditions are improving but caution is still needed.
3. **Flood Warning.** Issued when flooding is a definite reality. It stays in effect in later bulletins for as long as affected areas are flooded and dangers remain. It states the precautions that residents and the community must take.

Bulletins move up or down these categories as conditions change. An Outlook may be followed by another Outlook, an Advisory, or a Warning.

## What to do before a flood

Source: PAGASA, Learning Tools: Floods (Flood Safety Rules)

- Find out how often your location is likely to flood.
- Know your community's flood warning system, and make sure your family knows it too.
- Keep informed about daily weather conditions.
- Designate an evacuation area for your family and livestock.
- Assign family members instructions and responsibilities according to an evacuation plan.
- Keep a stock of food that needs little cooking and no refrigeration, since electric power may be interrupted.
- Keep a battery-powered radio, flashlight with spare batteries, emergency cooking equipment, candles, matches, and a first aid kit within easy reach.
- Store supplies and household items above the expected flood level.
- Securely anchor weak dwellings and loose items.

## What to do when a flood warning is issued, or water is rising

Source: PAGASA, Learning Tools: Floods (Flood Safety Rules, "When Warned of Flood")

- Watch for rapidly rising floodwater.
- Listen to the radio for emergency instructions.
- If you need to evacuate, move to a safe area before floodwater cuts off access.
- Store drinking water in containers, because water service may be interrupted.
- Move household belongings to upper levels.
- Move livestock to higher ground.
- Turn off electricity at the main switch before evacuating, and lock your house.
- If your house is expected to flood, move to higher ground.
- Move electrical appliances to upper floors.
- If electrical lines and outlets will be submerged, switch off the power.

## What to do during a flood

Source: PAGASA, Learning Tools: Floods (Flood Safety Rules, "During the Flood")

- Avoid areas subject to sudden flooding.
- Do not try to cross a river or flowing stream where the water is above knee level.
- Beware of water-covered roads and bridges.
- Avoid unnecessary exposure to the elements.
- Do not swim or boat in swollen rivers.
- Avoid wading in floodwater. This applies especially to children.
- Eat only well-cooked food, and protect leftovers from contamination.
- Drink only clean water, preferably boiled.

## What to do after a flood

Source: PAGASA, Learning Tools: Floods (Flood Safety Rules, "After the Flood")

- Re-enter your home carefully. Use flashlights, not lanterns or torches, because flammable materials may be inside.
- Watch for fire hazards such as broken wires.
- Do not eat food or drink water until it has been checked for floodwater contamination.
- Report broken utility lines (electricity, water, gas, telephone) to the proper authorities.
- Do not turn on the main switch or use appliances and equipment until a competent electrician has checked them.
- Consult health authorities about immunization requirements.
- Stay out of disaster areas. Your presence may hamper rescue and emergency operations.

## What residents can do to reduce flooding

Source: PAGASA, Learning Tools: Floods (Things One Can Do to Mitigate Floods)

- Do not throw garbage into esteros and rivers.
- Do not throw plastic wrappers or other trash anywhere they can clog or block drainage.
- Help clean up your neighborhood.
- Support community activities that reduce flooding.
- Report illegal logging and kaingin (slash-and-burn clearing).
- Report illegal construction of fishponds and other structures in waterways.
- Support rules that regulate the cutting of trees.
